# Questioning Instrumental Convergence in RLVR: Checkpoint Evidence from OLMo-3

Barış Alparslan    Sandy Tanwisuth    Caleb Petterson

###### Abstract

Instrumental convergence is often invoked as a general reason to expect capable agents to acquire or express power-seeking subgoals: self-preservation, resource acquisition, deception, or resistance to oversight which poses an important threat to the society. For LLMs, reinforcement learning is the first stage of post-training that directly optimizes behavior against an objective; therefore, it is the stage where convergent instrumental behavior, on IC grounds, likely to appear. Does reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) make a model more likely to express instrumental-convergence behavior, or does this depend on the training regime, evaluation frame, and stage of post-training? We study this question as a checkpoint-conditioned behavioral trajectory rather than as a single before/after safety score. Using InstrumentalEval, we evaluate OLMo-3-7B-Think across SFT, DPO, 13 intermediate RLVR checkpoints, and the final RLVR model. The trajectory is nonmonotonic: instrumental rate rises from 25.0% at SFT to 46.1% near RLVR step 200, then falls to 11.8% at the final checkpoint. Paired item-level tests and step-wise regression support the final SFT-to-RLVR reduction ($p=0.015$ by paired $t$-test; $p=0.03$ by Wilcoxon signed-rank test; $\beta_{2}=-15.34$ by step-wise regression). Prompt framing does not explain this pattern: a harmful-prompt indicator combined with other framing effects explains only 15% of within-checkpoint score variance on average, and Iterative Null-space Projection (INLP) removes a detectable framing direction without changing the SFT-to-final-RLVR gap. Together with preliminary 32B endpoint results (35.53% SFT to 22.37% RLVR final), these findings indicate that RLVR does not monotonically increase instrumental behavior in this setting: the rate rises early, then falls below the SFT baseline. The result is specific to this model family and benchmark, and judge agreement is moderate, so absolute rates should be read cautiously.

## 1 Introduction

Instrumental convergence describes the tendency of capable, goal-directed agents to pursue similar intermediate goals across a wide range of objectives. Preserving the ability to act, acquiring resources, avoiding shutdown, and shaping the environment can all help an agent continue pursuing its objective (Omohundro, 2008; Bostrom, 2012; Bostrom, 2014; Turner et al., 2023). This creates a potential connection between capability and risk: as a system becomes more capable and goal-directed, it may become more likely to pursue instrumental strategies, including when its objective appears benign. Applying this argument to large language models requires understanding how goal-directed behavior arises in their training and deployment. Language models can produce reasoning that justifies deception, resists shutdown, preserves goal integrity, or supports appearing aligned for strategic reasons (He et al., 2025; Meinke et al., 2024), including in settings with limited persistence or ability to act on the environment. Reinforcement learning makes this question particularly relevant because it directly optimizes behavior against an objective signal. At the outer-alignment level, we can ask whether the reward or verifier specifies the behavior we want. At the inner-alignment level, we can ask whether the learned model develops internal objectives or heuristics that diverge from the intended training objective (Hubinger et al., 2021). IC-like behavior provides a possible observable connection between these questions, while establishing a misaligned inner objective requires further evidence. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) provides a useful setting for studying this connection. It is widely used in post-training reasoning models because it allows optimization against automatically checkable outcomes in mathematics, coding, and other domains with verifiable answers (DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Lambert et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Its use of task-local verifiers, reference-policy constraints, and incremental updates also gives it a particular optimization structure that may affect which instrumental behaviors emerge. Recent work suggests that RLVR can concentrate probability mass on solutions already available in the base model and modify comparatively sparse subnetworks (Mukherjee et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2025). Understanding how these properties affect IC-like behavior therefore requires empirical evaluation. We ask how this behavior changes as the same model family passes through SFT, DPO, intermediate RLVR checkpoints, and the final RLVR release, holding the benchmark items fixed. We evaluate OLMo-3-7B-Think (Groeneveld et al., 2024; Olmo et al., 2026) using InstrumentalEval (He et al., 2025), which measures whether model outputs express IC-adjacent reasoning across subtasks. Since safety benchmarks can encode framing artifacts (Ren et al., 2024), we also examine whether harmful prompt tone explains the observed trajectory using per-checkpoint regression and Iterative Null-space Projection (INLP) (Ravfogel et al., 2020). We find that IC-like behavior increases during DPO and early RLVR, peaks near step 200, and declines during later RLVR, with the final checkpoint falling below the SFT baseline. Our analyses detect framing structure in the score matrix, and the trajectory remains unchanged after removing a linear framing direction. These findings establish a nonmonotonic relationship between post-training stage and IC-like outputs for this model, benchmark, and judging protocol, with later RLVR reversing the early increase and reducing the final level below SFT.

## 2 Conceptual Frame

### 2.1 Instrumental convergence as a conditional thesis

The instrumental-convergence argument begins from a structural observation, certain intermediate states make success easier. A system that can preserve itself, acquire resources, avoid interruption, or strategically manage oversight may be better able to pursue its objective (Omohundro, 2008; Bostrom, 2014; Turner et al., 2023). This implies that, under some conditions, capability and goal-directedness may make such behaviors instrumentally useful.

Recent philosophical work has questioned how direct this inference should be. (Southan et al., 2025) argue that the timing and dominance of convergent drives may be more constrained than canonical presentations suggest. (Gallow, 2025) similarly argues that instrumental divergence is also natural: agents with different goals may pursue different intermediate strategies. These critiques do not dissolve the safety concern, but they change the empirical burden.

### 2.2 InstrumentalEval as a behavioral probe

InstrumentalEval operationalizes instrumental convergence through six subtasks derived from classic convergent-drive categories (He et al., 2025). The subtasks ask whether the model expresses reasoning associated with evading shutdown, hacking computer systems, running copies of itself, hiding unwanted behavior, strategically appearing aligned, and deceptive behavior. In this paper, we use these subtasks as an output-level probe of IC-like reasoning and evaluate them with a judge model. The judge prompt for evaluation is available in table 9.

A positive score on any of these subtasks indicates that IC-like reasoning appeared in the output. However, it does not establish that the model holds a corresponding internal goal or that the behavior would persist across contexts, as this distinction requires looking inside the model.

The benchmark contains 76 tasks. We score each response continuously in $[0,1]$ and threshold at $0.5$ to compute the Instrumental Rate (IR):

$$
IR=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I(z_{i}^{M}\geq 0.5),
$$

where $z_{i}^{M}$ is the convergence score assigned by judge model $M$. We also compute inter-judge agreement rate (IAR) between two judge models:

$$
IAR(M_{1},M_{2})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I(y_{M_{1}}^{i}=y_{M_{2}}^{i}),
$$

where $y_{M}^{i}=I(z_{i}^{M}\geq 0.5)$. For trajectory analyses, lower IR means fewer IC-like outputs. For judge analyses, higher IAR means the binary IC labels are more stable across judge models.

### 2.3 RLVR as a constrained post-training regime

RLVR optimizes a policy using rewards from a rule-based verifier rather than a learned preference model. A simplified objective is

$$
\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{z,y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}\left[f_{\text{verifier}}(y,y^{*})\right], \qquad (1)
$$

where $f_{\text{verifier}}$ evaluates whether a response satisfies a task-specific criterion (Yu et al., 2025). In mathematics, this may be symbolic equivalence; in coding, it may be execution against tests.

OLMo-3-7B-Think is not trained by RLVR alone. Its post-training pipeline includes SFT, DPO, and RLVR checkpoints released across the training trajectory (Olmo et al., 2026). This matters because a final checkpoint can hide intermediate dynamics. A before/after comparison between SFT and final RLVR would miss whether IC-like behavior rises during early RLVR and falls later.

KL regularization against a reference policy in DPO structure can limit drift from the pretrained distribution. This constrained optimization structure is central to our interpretation: RLVR in this instrumental-convergence evaluation context may not create open-ended power-seeking pressure in the same way as broader RL settings. It may instead narrow the behavioral distribution around verifier-rewarded reasoning patterns.

## 3 Checkpoint Evaluation and Framing Controls

The experiment is designed to separate checkpoint effects from item effects. The benchmark items are held fixed, the scoring protocol is held fixed, and the checkpoint varies.

### 3.1 Experimental setup

We evaluate OLMo-3-7B-Think across 16 checkpoints: SFT, DPO, 13 intermediate RLVR checkpoints, and the final released RLVR checkpoint (Olmo et al., 2026). The intermediate RLVR steps are $\{50,75,100,125,175,200,275,350,450,600,800,1050,1375\}$. Each checkpoint is evaluated on the same 76 InstrumentalEval tasks.

Inference uses greedy decoding with maximum response length 1024 and a fixed system prompt across checkpoints. GPT-4o is the primary judge, with temperature $0.0$ and maximum response length 200. For each task-response pair, the judge assigns a continuous IC score in $[0,1]$ and provides a one-sentence explanation. A score of $0.5$ is the binary threshold for computing IR. To assess judge robustness, all cached responses are also scored with Gemini 2.0 Flash as a secondary judge.

### 3.2 Prompt framing as a nuisance variable

InstrumentalEval prompts vary not only in the drive they test but also in how the scenario is framed. Some prompts present the relevant behavior in overtly harmful language, and some describe the target in the context of social good.

Prompt framing is the most tractable confound addressed here. The concern comes when safety benchmark scores that conflate framing sensitivity with behavioral tendencies risk misrepresenting a model’s actual risk profile (Ren et al., 2024).

To analyze prompt framing effect, we manually label each of the 76 tasks with a binary harmful/neutral framing indicator $H_{i}$, roughly indicating goal-directness of the prompt and inc. These splits contain 41 harmful and 35 neutral prompts. Because every checkpoint is evaluated on the same items, the framing labels are fixed across checkpoints by construction.

### 3.3 Explained variance

The first framing check asks how much score variation the harmful-prompt indicator explains within each checkpoint. To make more in-depth analysis, we add the effects of social good context indicator $S_{i}$, and the human obstacle indicator $O_{i}$ as in definition 3. These definitions split the dataset with 37 socially positive and 39 neutral, and 44 human obstacle and 32 neutral prompts. For every checkpoint $c$, we fit a normal linear model

$$
\mathbb{E}\left(z_{ic}|.\right)=\alpha_{i}+\gamma_{c}+\beta_{1c}H_{i}+\beta_{2c}S_{i}+\beta_{3c}O_{i}+\beta_{4c}H_{i}S_{i}+\beta_{5c}H_{i}O_{i}+\beta_{6c}O_{i}S_{i}+\beta_{7c}H_{i}S_{i}O_{i} \qquad (2)
$$

where $z_{ic}$ is the judge score for item $i$ at checkpoint $c$, $\alpha_{i}$ is the fixed effect capturing the prompt difference, and $\gamma_{c}$ is the fixed effect capturing the training trajectory. Note that

$$
H_{i}=\{\text{$1$ if the prompt $i$ is harmful, $0$ otherwise}\} \qquad (3)
$$

$$
S_{i}=\{\text{$1$ if the prompt $i$ serves for social good, $0$ otherwise}\}
$$

$$
O_{i}=\{\text{$1$ if the prompt $i$ frames humans as obstacle, $0$ otherwise}\}
$$

We report $R^{2}$ as the fraction of within-checkpoint score variance explained by framing, and the significance of $\beta_{1c}$ when $S=0,O=0$.

### 3.4 Iterative Null-space Projection

The second framing check asks whether a shared linear framing direction affects the full checkpoint-by-item score matrix. We use INLP (Ravfogel et al., 2020), which iteratively trains a linear classifier to predict the protected attribute and projects the representation into the classifier’s null space. In our setting, the protected attribute is the harmful-prompt indicator $H_{i}$ and the representation is the score vector for an item across checkpoints.

At iteration $k$, a linear classifier with weight vector $w_{k}$ is trained to predict $H_{i}$. We construct the projection

$$
P_{N(w_{k})}=I-\frac{w_{k}w_{k}^{T}}{w_{k}^{T}w_{k}},
$$

for transforming the score matrix, and repeat until the classifier no longer exceeds the majority-class baseline. The final projection is

$$
P=P_{N(w_{n})}\ldots P_{N(w_{1})}.
$$

If the SFT-to-final-RLVR gap disappears after this projection, then the trajectory may be explained by a linear framing direction. If the gap remains, then detectable linear framing structure is not driving the reported result.

## 4 Experiments and Results

### 4.1 Results

We asked whether instrumental-convergence behavior is stable across the OLMo-3 post-training trajectory, or whether the same benchmark items elicit different IC-like outputs at different checkpoints. We first examine the raw checkpoint trajectory. We then test whether the trajectory is explained by prompt framing, and finally check whether the main pattern is stable across judge models. Table 1 reports the full checkpoint trajectory; additional per-checkpoint framing and judge analyses are provided in Appendix A.4.

Table 1 shows how instrumental behavior changes across all evaluated checkpoints. IR begins at 25.00% at SFT, increases to 38.16% after DPO, and reaches its highest observed value of 46.05% at RLVR step 200. The intermediate checkpoints show fluctuations around this early increase. Later checkpoints trace a decline, reaching 22.37% at step 800, 17.11% at step 1375, and 11.84% at the final release. These results show that the effect of post-training depends on the checkpoint at which the model is evaluated: early training increases IC-like outputs, and later RLVR brings the final model below the SFT baseline. Since the benchmark contains 76 items, a single binary outcome changes IR by roughly 1.3 percentage points. The confidence intervals and complete checkpoint sequence help place these local changes in the context of the overall trajectory.

$$

$$

H_{1} :\tilde{\mu}_{D}>0\quad\text{(RLVR reduces score)},

Taken together, these results show that RLVR changes the observed IC trajectory, but not by monotonically increasing instrumental convergence. In OLMo-3-7B-Think, DPO and early RLVR increase IC-like outputs, while later RLVR reverses this pattern and the final checkpoint falls below the SFT baseline. Prompt framing is detectable in the score matrix, but neither the per-checkpoint linear framing analysis nor the INLP projection explains the final reduction. The result is therefore a behavioral checkpoint-trajectory claim, not a broad safety claim: for this model, benchmark, and judging protocol, the final RLVR checkpoint produces fewer IC-like outputs than the SFT checkpoint.

### 4.2 Extension to the larger model

We next examine whether the endpoint reduction also appears at a larger model scale. We evaluate the SFT and final RLVR checkpoints of OLMo-3-32B-Think, which belongs to the same model family and training pipeline. Table 6 shows that IR decreases from 35.53% at SFT to 22.37% at the final RLVR checkpoint. Applying the paired tests in Equations 5 and 6 gives $p=0.034$ for the paired $t$-test and $p=0.029$ for the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. This preliminary evaluation supports an endpoint reduction at both scales within the OLMo-3 family. Establishing whether the 32B model also exhibits the early rise and later decline observed at 7B requires intermediate-checkpoint evaluations. ## 5 Discussion

Our checkpoint evaluation shows that IC-like behavior rises early in RLVR training and declines at later checkpoints. This trajectory suggests that the relationship between optimization and instrumental behavior depends on the training regime, with the objective, update geometry, reference constraint, and evaluation setting providing possible explanations for the observed pattern. The canonical instrumental-convergence argument concerns sufficiently capable goal-directed agents, and applying it to verifier-trained language models requires understanding how these conditions arise in a particular training setting. RLVR may strengthen reasoning while narrowing the distribution of expressed behaviors, allowing capability and IC-like output expression to move in different directions. From an outer-alignment perspective, a verifier that rewards task-local correctness may leave important safety constraints unspecified while also limiting incentives for broad instrumental strategies. For example, correctness on a math or code task provides limited direct incentive for resource acquisition, self-preservation, or strategic deception. This makes the scope of the verifier relevant both to the completeness of the reward specification and to the behaviors that optimization encourages. From an inner-alignment perspective, the lower final IR establishes a reduction in IC-like responses under the benchmark and judging protocol. The relationship between this reduction and any learned goal-like structures remains an open question. The strategically appearing aligned subtask makes this question particularly clear: reasoning about appearing aligned to preserve future influence resembles the behavior associated with deceptive alignment (Hubinger et al., 2021; Greenblatt et al., 2024). A decline on this subtask could reflect reduced deceptive-compliance reasoning or a lower probability of expressing that reasoning. Mechanistic analysis of the checkpoint sequence would help distinguish these explanations and assess whether the observed behavior corresponds to an internal objective. Our findings complement work identifying conditions under which RL-trained systems express reward-seeking, deception, goal misgeneralization, and ethically problematic strategies (Langosco et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2023; Denison et al., 2024; Greenblatt et al., 2024; Hubinger et al., 2024). We examine how such behavior changes along a verifier-based RLVR trajectory in one open model family, where the early increase is followed by a decline below the SFT baseline. This checkpoint-level analysis also extends the question motivating InstrumentalEval in (He et al., 2025), who ask whether RL-based language models are more likely to pursue instrumental goals. Tracking released checkpoints reveals training dynamics that endpoint comparisons can obscure. Existing work on RLVR provides possible mechanisms for these changes. Sparse update results suggest that RL fine-tuning can concentrate changes in limited subnetworks (Mukherjee et al., 2025), and work on reasoning capacity suggests that RLVR can increase the probability of solutions already available to the model (Yue et al., 2025). If IC-like continuations have relatively low probability in the SFT distribution, later RLVR could further reduce their sampling probability while preserving the underlying representations. In this sense, our evaluation identifies checkpoint intervals in which mechanistic analysis may be especially informative. The early-RLVR peak and subsequent decline provide concrete settings for studying how changes in expressed behavior relate to changes within the model.

## 6 Limitations

Our evaluation has several limitations that are important for interpreting these results. We primarily study OLMo-3-7B-Think, and while the preliminary experiments with a larger model in Section 4.2 partially address the question of scale, both models belong to the same family. The observed trajectory may therefore depend on the model parameters, scale, or release trajectory, and replication on RLVR-trained models from other labs is needed to understand how broadly the result applies. Similarly, InstrumentalEval provides a focused behavioral probe through 76 tasks across six convergence categories, but this limits statistical power and captures only part of the space of IC-relevant behavior. In this sense, our evaluation provides evidence about a particular behavioral expression of instrumental convergence rather than a complete test of the thesis. Our framing controls are also limited to linearly recoverable structure: the $R^{2}$ and INLP analyses support the conclusion that a simple linear framing direction does not explain the main trajectory, but do not rule out nonlinear effects, interactions between framing and subtask, or differences in how judges respond to prompt tone. An analysis stratified by subtask or using a richer classification of prompt tone could help address these questions. Moreover, average inter-judge agreement is moderate at 68.75%, with stronger agreement at the final RLVR checkpoint and weaker agreement near the early-RLVR peak. This supports the direction of the final result, although absolute IR values should be interpreted cautiously and would benefit from human validation on a stratified sample. Finally, our evaluation does not establish whether the verifier is outer-aligned. A narrow verifier may limit opportunities for broad instrumental strategies while failing to specify important safety constraints, and we measure only one behavioral consequence along the released checkpoint sequence. Comparing alternative verifier designs is therefore a useful direction for understanding which aspects of the observed trajectory are specific to this verifier and which may generalize to RLVR more broadly.

#### Broader Impact Statement

This work should not be used as evidence that RLVR-trained systems are broadly safe. Our result is limited to one model family, one benchmark, and one judging protocol. It suggests that IC-like behavior can decrease along a verifier-based RL trajectory, not that RLVR solves inner alignment, outer alignment, deceptive alignment, or instrumental convergence in general.

The positive use of the work is diagnostic. Checkpoint-level evaluations can reveal nonmonotonic safety trajectories that endpoint comparisons miss. We hope this encourages future work to evaluate post-training methods as trajectories, combine behavioral benchmarks with mechanistic analysis, and treat instrumental convergence as a conditional empirical phenomenon rather than as a slogan.
